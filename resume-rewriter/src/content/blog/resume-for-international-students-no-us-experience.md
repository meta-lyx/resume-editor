Many international students arrive with good experience: an internship in Shanghai, research in Bangalore, a startup job in São Paulo. Then they hear that US recruiters want "US experience" and assume it doesn't count.

It does. US employers care about what you did and what changed because of it. Where you did it matters much less, as long as the reader can understand it. This guide shows how to turn the experience you have into a resume that works in the US market.

## Your home-country experience counts. Explain it

The problem is rarely the experience. It's that a US recruiter can't place it. They don't know the company, the title doesn't map to a US role, and the bullets describe duties instead of results.

Fix all three:

**1. Add context to unfamiliar companies.** One short phrase does it:

> **Business Analyst Intern**, Pinduoduo (e-commerce platform, 800M+ users) · Shanghai · Jun 2024 – Sep 2024

> **Research Assistant**, Indian Institute of Science (India's top-ranked research university) · Bengaluru · 2023 – 2024

**2. Use US-equivalent titles.** If your title was literal or unusual, use the one a US company would give the same role. Keep it accurate. "Data Analyst Intern" for analytics work is fine; "Data Scientist" for a role that was mostly reporting is not.

**3. Write results, not duties.** Every bullet: verb, what you did, and a number where you can.

> Built a Python script to clean 2M supplier records weekly, cutting the team's reporting time from 2 days to 3 hours.

A recruiter who has never heard of your company will still understand that bullet.

## Turn class projects into experience

If you lack internships, a **Projects** section can carry your resume. Treat each project like a job: a title, context, and two or three results-focused bullets.

> **Churn Prediction Model** | Course project, NYU Stern · Python, scikit-learn, SQL · Fall 2025
> - Built a gradient-boosting model on 120K telecom customer records to predict churn (AUC 0.87, best of 34 teams)
> - Presented retention recommendations to a guest panel from a national carrier; estimated $1.2M annual savings

What makes a project count:

- **Real data or a real client.** Industry-sponsored capstones and consulting projects are especially strong.
- **Tools named the way job postings name them.** If the job asks for Tableau, say Tableau.
- **A result, rank or audience.** "Best of 34 teams", "used by 200 classmates", "presented to the client's VP".

Personal projects count too: a published app, an analysis on Kaggle, a GitHub repo with real users.

## Use every US experience you do have

Small US roles show you can work in an American workplace. Don't leave them off because they seem minor:

- **On-campus jobs**: library assistant, dining services supervisor, IT help desk. Show reliability, customer service and responsibility.
- **Research or teaching assistant roles**: often directly relevant to technical jobs.
- **Student organization leadership**: "Led a 12-person team to organize a career fair for 600 students and 25 employers."
- **Case competitions and hackathons**: name the sponsor and your placement.
- **Volunteering**: skilled volunteering for a nonprofit counts as experience.

Remember work authorization limits: on F-1, off-campus work generally requires CPT or OPT. On-campus jobs, class projects and unpaid academic work are the usual safe routes. Check with your DSO before taking any off-campus role.

## Put the most relevant section first

US resumes are scanned top to bottom in seconds. Put your strongest relevant material near the top:

1. **Education**: degree, school, expected graduation, GPA if 3.3/4.0 or higher, relevant coursework (one line)
2. **Experience**: home-country internships and US campus roles, most relevant first
3. **Projects**: if they're stronger than your experience, move them above it
4. **Skills**: tools and languages, including your native language

Keep it to one page.

## Show the skills US recruiters screen for

International students often underplay skills US employers search for:

- **Languages.** "Mandarin (native)" or "Hindi (native)" is a real asset for companies with global teams or customers.
- **Cross-market knowledge.** Experience with Chinese, Indian or Latin American markets is valuable for companies expanding there.
- **Technical tools.** Name them specifically: "SQL (BigQuery)", "Excel (pivot tables, VLOOKUP)", "Figma".

Then match each application's vocabulary. Recruiters search their applicant tracking system for the skills in the job posting. Paste the job description and your resume into the [free resume keyword matcher](/tools/resume-keyword-matcher) to see what's missing.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Leaving off home-country experience** because it "isn't US". Include it with context.
- **Including a photo, birth date or marital status.** Standard elsewhere, a red flag in the US. See the [US resume format guide](/blog/us-resume-format-for-international-students).
- **Translating bullets word for word** from another language. Rewrite them in US resume style; [here is how for Chinese resumes](/blog/translate-chinese-resume-to-english).
- **Listing every course.** One line of the most relevant courses is enough.
- **Using a non-US phone number.** Get a US number before you apply.
