Sending the same resume to every opening is the fastest way to apply and one of the slowest ways to get interviews. Recruiters skim, and most employers run applications through an applicant tracking system (ATS) that lets them search and rank candidates by the skills a role requires. A resume that speaks the language of the job description is easier for both to say yes to.

Tailoring does not mean rewriting your resume from scratch for every application. It means keeping a strong master resume and making a few targeted changes each time. Here is a method that takes about 15 minutes per job once you have done it a couple of times.

## 1. Start from a master resume

Keep one longer document that holds everything you might want to show: every role, project, tool, certification and result, with numbers wherever you have them. You will never send this version. It is the pantry you cook from.

Each tailored resume is a selection from the master, reordered and reworded for one role, and trimmed to one page (or two if you have a decade or more of relevant experience).

## 2. Read the job description like a recruiter

Copy the posting into a document and mark it up. You are looking for four things:

- **Hard requirements.** Years of experience, degrees, certifications, work authorization, specific tools. These are often phrased as "required" or "must have".
- **Core skills.** The technical and functional skills that appear more than once, in the title, or near the top. Repetition is a signal of priority.
- **Outcomes.** What the person in this role is expected to deliver: "reduce churn", "ship features weekly", "own the quarterly close".
- **Exact wording.** Note the employer's terms. If they say "stakeholder management" and your resume says "worked with partners", you are describing the same thing in a way that will not match a search.

A useful habit: list the top eight to ten skills and outcomes in order of importance. That list drives every change below.

## 3. Rewrite your summary for this role

The two to three lines at the top are the most-read part of your resume, so make them about the job in front of you. Name the role, your most relevant experience, and one or two of the employer's priorities that you can genuinely back up.

**Generic:**
> Motivated professional with experience in a variety of software projects and a passion for technology.

**Tailored for a backend engineering role that stresses APIs and PostgreSQL:**
> Backend engineer with 5 years building REST APIs in Node.js and TypeScript. Led a PostgreSQL migration serving 2M daily users and cut p95 latency by 40%.

Every claim in the tailored version should be something you can talk about in an interview.

## 4. Reorder and rewrite your bullets

Within each role, move the bullets that match your top-ten list to the top. Then rewrite them so the relevant skill and the result are obvious at a glance. A reliable pattern is **action + what you did + measurable result**, using the employer's wording where it is accurate.

**Before:**
> Responsible for the reporting dashboard and helping the sales team.

**After (for a role that emphasizes "data visualization" and "stakeholder management"):**
> Built a Tableau sales dashboard used by 40 account managers; gathered requirements from sales leadership and cut weekly reporting time from 6 hours to 1.

If a bullet has nothing to do with the target role, cut it or shorten it to make room for one that does.

## 5. Mirror the language without copying it

Use the job description's terms for skills you actually have. If the posting says "CI/CD" and you set up GitHub Actions pipelines, say "CI/CD (GitHub Actions)". If it spells out "Search Engine Optimization (SEO)", include both the full term and the acronym once, because people and software may search for either.

What not to do:

- Do not paste requirements you do not meet. It may get you past a filter, but it will fall apart in the first interview.
- Do not hide keywords in white text or a tiny font. Recruiters see the parsed text, and it reads as a red flag.
- Do not repeat the same keyword in every bullet. Two or three natural mentions of a core skill is plenty.

Our guide to [resume keywords](/blog/resume-keywords-from-job-description) goes deeper on finding and placing them.

## 6. Adjust your skills section

Make sure the skills section lists the core tools and methods from the posting that you can honestly claim, in roughly the order the employer prioritized them. Remove skills that are irrelevant to this role; they dilute the ones that matter.

## 7. Check format before you send

Tailored content only helps if it can be read. Use standard section headings ("Experience", "Education", "Skills"), avoid putting key information in tables, text boxes, headers or footers, and export a text-based PDF unless the application asks for Word. Our guide to [ATS-friendly resume formatting](/blog/ats-friendly-resume-format) covers the details.

## The 15-minute tailoring checklist

1. Mark up the job description and list the top eight to ten skills and outcomes.
2. Rewrite the summary around the role and your two strongest matching results.
3. Move matching bullets to the top of each relevant role.
4. Rewrite two or three bullets with the employer's wording and a measurable result.
5. Add any missing core skills you genuinely have; remove irrelevant ones.
6. Spell out acronyms once alongside the short form.
7. Export a clean, text-based PDF and name it clearly, for example `Jane-Doe-Backend-Engineer.pdf`.
