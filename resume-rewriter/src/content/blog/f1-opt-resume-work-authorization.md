Most international students ask the same question before applying: do I put my visa status on my resume? The short answer: you don't have to, but a single honest line can help, especially if you are eligible for STEM OPT.

This guide covers when to mention it, how to word it, and how to handle the sponsorship question that comes later. It is general information, not legal advice. For your own situation, talk to your school's Designated School Official (DSO) or an immigration attorney.

## A quick refresher on the terms

- **F-1** is the student visa status. It doesn't by itself allow off-campus work.
- **CPT (Curricular Practical Training)** lets you work during your program when the work is part of your curriculum, such as a required or for-credit internship. Your school authorizes it.
- **OPT (Optional Practical Training)** lets you work in your field for up to 12 months per degree level, usually after graduation.
- **STEM OPT extension** adds 24 months for graduates of eligible STEM degrees, so up to 36 months in total. The employer must be enrolled in E-Verify and complete a training plan with you.
- **H-1B** is the common work visa after OPT. It requires employer sponsorship and is capped, with a lottery each year.

What US employers care about is simple: can you start when we need you, for how long, and will we need to sponsor a visa later?

## Do you have to put it on your resume?

No. A resume is not a legal document, and many applications ask about work authorization separately. Leaving it off is not dishonest.

But recruiters often scan resumes for exactly this. If they see a foreign undergraduate degree and no US address, some will assume you need sponsorship now, and skip you. A clear line can stop that wrong assumption.

## When mentioning it helps

Mention work authorization when it is a **strength**:

- **You have STEM OPT eligibility.** Up to three years of work authorization without sponsorship is a meaningful selling point for an entry-level hire.
- **The role is an internship and you have CPT.** It tells the employer there is no paperwork burden on them.
- **The company is known to hire international students**, or the posting explicitly welcomes them.
- **You don't need sponsorship at all**, for example as a permanent resident or a citizen who studied abroad. State it plainly to prevent a wrong assumption.

## When to leave it off

- The employer clearly does not sponsor and you will need sponsorship. The application will ask; your resume doesn't need to lead with it.
- You are applying for roles that require US citizenship (many defense and government jobs). These are generally not open to F-1 students, so don't spend applications on them.
- Your authorization is complicated. A single unclear line is worse than none.

## How to word it

Put one line at the end of your header, or at the bottom of your Education or Skills section. Keep it factual and short.

For STEM OPT-eligible graduates:

> Work authorization: F-1 OPT, eligible for 24-month STEM extension (36 months total), no sponsorship required until 2029

For students seeking internships:

> Work authorization: F-1 CPT available for Summer 2027 internship

For non-STEM graduates on OPT:

> Work authorization: F-1 OPT, authorized to work in the US through Aug 2027

For permanent residents or citizens:

> Work authorization: US permanent resident, no sponsorship required

Avoid vague phrases such as "visa status: F-1", which signals a question without answering it. And never overstate. Don't write "no sponsorship required" if you will need H-1B sponsorship within the period the employer cares about.

## The sponsorship question on applications

Many applications ask: *"Will you now or in the future require sponsorship for employment visa status (e.g. H-1B)?"*

Answer truthfully. If you are on OPT or STEM OPT and expect to need an H-1B to keep working, the accurate answer is usually **yes**, even though you need nothing today. A false answer can come out later in onboarding or background checks, and the consequences can include a withdrawn offer.

Two things make "yes" less damaging:

1. **Apply where sponsorship is realistic.** Larger companies, universities, research institutions and many tech, finance and consulting firms sponsor regularly. Public H-1B filing data, searchable by employer on several free sites, shows who has sponsored for roles like yours.
2. **Lead with your OPT window.** In a cover letter or recruiter call, state the fact simply: "I'm authorized to work for up to three years under STEM OPT, so there's no immediate paperwork."

## How to talk about it in interviews

When a recruiter asks about status, answer in two sentences:

> "I'm on F-1 OPT and eligible for the STEM extension, so I'm authorized to work for up to three years. Longer term I would need H-1B sponsorship."

Clear, confident and complete. The recruiter's job is to know whether the process will be a problem; a clear answer shows it won't surprise them.

## Make the rest of your resume carry the weight

Work authorization rarely gets you an interview on its own. Relevant experience, results and the right keywords do. Before each application:

- Follow the [US resume format for international students](/blog/us-resume-format-for-international-students): no photo, no birth date, one page.
- Run your resume through the [free resume keyword matcher](/tools/resume-keyword-matcher) against the job posting.
- Check readability and US conventions with the [free ATS resume checker](/tools/ats-resume-checker), which also notes whether you've included a work-authorization line.

Immigration rules change. Confirm the current details of OPT and STEM OPT with your DSO before you write the line on your resume.
